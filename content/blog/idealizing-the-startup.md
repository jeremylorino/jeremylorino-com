Recently my sister was asked by a potential employer to identify the *most innovative startup*. When she asked my opinion I immediately responded with:

> It is easy to start a company; it is difficult to keep a company running.

So why do we idolize these fly-by-night companies that are “innovating”, “disrupting”, or “revolutionizing” industries?

## The Good Company

In a small company there is no ambiguity as to whether an employer cares about you as an employee. The company is small enough that you can see the signs; directly or indirectly. The larger the company, the blurrier this becomes.

Larger companies have employees, managers, leads that care about employees of the company. And every so often a company will do something that will lead one to believe they either care or they do not care about their employees. But these events are few and sometimes forced or ambiguous; leading employees to question the positive messages and seek the “hidden” agenda.


In between these positive and negative messages coming from companies is a void. In a healthy company, this void between messages is bridged by the faith employees have in their employer. In less healthy companies, this void fuels the negative. You could see how this could perpetuate an increase in employees doubting their employer and visa versa.

> Being a good company is the difference between life and death when things go wrong. — Ben Horowitz, The Hard Thing About Hard Things

Throughout history the relationship between employees and employers as been a battle. For a decade the employer takes advantage of employees. Then for a decade change takes place to tip the scales. Then everyone is happy. Then eventually the employees begin to take advantage of their employer and the cycle starts over.

## The 100 Year-Old Startup

[Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs), a decedent of [Ma Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_System), over the course of 50 years helped define what it means for a company to be innovative. Yes, Bell Systems — aka Ma Bell — maintained a large group of companies that were at times considered a monopoly by the United States. One must ask — how can a company exist that **truly** creates new things, or *innovates*, without maintaining the overwhelming share of a market or an industry?

The answer — they created the market or the industry*. *This is a “zero-to-one” situation; a concept explained by Peter Thiel in his book [Zero to One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_to_One).

Bell Labs created, time and time again, things we all take for granted today; including local and global cellular voice/video communication, the silicon chip, solid state storage, solar cells and numerous others.

## Today’s Startup

Today we generally define a startup as a company that was recently founded and on the path to an IPO or acquisition. Usually an infant of a company with a short-term idea fueled by the delusion of becoming an overnight billionaire.

These companies are *innovating*, *disrupting*, and *revolutionizing *nothing; at least not yet.

Just as we admire the zeal of a young naive woman or man; we have come to admire the same of the newly founded companies. This admiration has embedded the idea that the startup company is the only vessel for innovation.

When I survey the landscape in search of teams that have created value, I look to the wise Tortoise; not the arrogant Hare. For one seeks long-lasting value; while the other seeks only the finish line. Yet having one without the other will destroy value.
